"Here is a picture: three guards on one wire are really one guard. So ask: where do the copies meet?

A lock has three guards. Each checks the code. But all three send their answer down one shared wire, and the door listens only to that wire. Make that one wire say the wrong thing for a moment, and three guards are ignored. In our toy picture, one flip beats all three.

The fix: give each guard its own wire. In our picture you now need three flips. On the project's small design that holds for the 8-light answer only; the separate accept flag falls to one flip.

Here is the twist, measured by a computer check on small designs. We rebuilt one three-guard design 32 ways. They come from one recipe, so they are not 32 independent designs. Twenty-six needed only two flips; six needed three. Count a flip on a joined wire once for every place it feeds, and all 32 needed three, again for the 8-light answer only. In the sources we read, we did not find a check of which way of counting matches real hardware.

So ask a supplier: where do the copies meet, how do you count a fault, and what is checked at the end?

We checked a small model on a computer. Nothing physical was done. It holds only for the final result word, from a known start, with inputs not attacked."
