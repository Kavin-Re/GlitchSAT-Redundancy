Break the lock

Goal: open the lock with the wrong code in no more flips than the best possible score (the fewest that can open it). Tap a round wire to flip it. A flip is a glitch that turns one 0 into 1, or one 1 into 0, for one clock tick. Picture story first

Illustration of the structure, not a result

The 8 lights are the lock’s answer (the “result word”). The lock opens only when all 8 are lit. A guard is a check that says “no” to a wrong code. A joined wire is one wire that several guards send their answers into. A bigger number of flips is better for whoever builds the lock.

    What you just saw

    • Backup guards that share one wire are one weak point toy picture. One flip on the shared wire beats all of them. (Measured: two checks into one shared wire fell to 1 flip, the same as one check.)
    • Give every guard its own wire and you need one flip per guard toy picture. Measured on the project’s small design: 3 flips for the result word; the separate “accepted” flag itself falls to 1 flip.
    • In the designs we tested (one recipe family), the exact wiring matters measured on small designs. Of 32 rebuilds of one small design, 26 needed 2 flips and 6 needed 3. Count a joined wire once for every place it feeds, and all 32 needed 3. The 32 come from one recipe on one design, so they are not 32 independent designs. In the sources we read we did not find a check of which way of counting is physical.

    What we did, and where it holds

    We checked a small model on a computer. Nothing physical was done. No real product was tested and none is shown to be weak.

    • We only asked whether the lock’s main answer (the 8 lights, the “result word”) can be forced. (See the caveat above the cards.)
    • We assumed we know how the lock starts and did not let the attacker change the code going in; only wires inside the lock can flip.
    • Direction of the glitch. A power dip makes outputs fall (1 to 0). A push the other way (0 to 1) breaks even the guarded designs in our model. Which is realistic depends on the attack.
    • We tried up to 3 flips. Nothing is said about 4 or more.
    Limits and evidence for engineers

    Labels: toy outcomes on this page are ILLUSTRATIVE and computed by the code on this page. “Computer check” numbers are MEASURED: results/reproduce_summary.json and results/reproduce/*.json (rows 1 to 4 and the “Wiring B” row), and docs/ENGINE_STATUS.md (the “Wiring A” row, conda). They are bounded: at most 3 modelled single-moment bit flips and 6 clock ticks, on small toy designs. “3” means found with 3 and not with 2; it does not say anything about 4 or more.

    Names: No backup = V0 naive. Two guards, one shared wire = V1 (duplicated check, AND into net ok). Three guards, own wires = V2 on the spike netlist. Wiring A = V2 re-synthesized by conda Yosys 0.38+92 (83 gates). Wiring B = V2 re-synthesized by apt Yosys 0.33 (85 or 81 gates). “Three guards, one shared wire” is toy only: no project design and no solver run.

    Simplifications: real netlists have 58 to 85 gates; the toy shows only the wires that matter. The shape of “Wiring B” (two joined wires covering all 8 lights) is an ILLUSTRATION: the solver found two gates feeding several result bits, and no cited result (research/c2/b2_scope gives only the minimum 2; research/c3/m1 has RTL and a script, no per-bit result) says they cover all 8, so the exact bits are UNVERIFIED. The “best possible score” is the toy’s own fewest-flips number, which matches the computer check where one was run. The toy does not flip the 8 lights themselves. Goal is the result word only (the accept flag falls to 1 flip, CT-1 in docs/dossier/CRITIQUE.md); start state known; input nets are not fault sites. A flip on a joined wire counts as one fault. Measured on one small design family: 26 of 32 rebuilds needed 2 flips and 6 needed 3; counting a wire once per pin it feeds, all 32 needed 3 (research/c2/devils_advocate/DA_CYCLE1.md, re-run from the same recipe list in research/c3/rep/REP_REPORT.md, so not independent designs; flips of only some branches of a wire were not tested; neither way of counting is shown to be physical). Not yet tested by real people. Nothing here shows that any real product is weak or that any tool makes a design secure. No data is collected; nothing leaves your browser.
