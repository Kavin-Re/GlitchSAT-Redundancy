## 1. What this project really is (5 lines)
1. A small, reproducible z3/Yosys-netlist tool that asks "how many modelled single-cycle bit flips make this output wrong", plus a measurement showing that the answer for the same Verilog is 1, 2 or 3 depending on netlist, scope and counting rule [S: B2 matrix; E2: V2 min 1/2/3 in 180/60/24 of 264 solved configs, 864 configs total].
2. Its surviving, defensible contribution is a sensitivity method: report the verdict as a tuple (design, netlist hash, goal/consumer, fault-site rule, init, counting rule), and the M1 finding that "flips needed" depends on how many result bits the consumer checks (V2 spike: 1 flip for 88/228 masks, 3 flips for 44/228) [S: M1 sec 2-4]. The M1 closed-form rule is exact on the spike V2 (255/255 masks) but FAILS on the apt-0.33 re-synthesis (68 mismatches) because ABC shares nodes [S: M1 sec 3]. So the "exact rule" is not yet a general rule.
3. It is a toy-scale tool: 8-bit compare designs, 6 cycles, k<=3; largest design run is a 2040-gate AES S-box DMR at k<=2, T=4, tens of seconds, k=3 does not finish in 250 s [S: R20 sec 5]. Prior art (SYNFI, FIRMER, k-FRP, uArchiFI) covers the base method; nothing is established as new; SYNFI already showed synthesis dependence 3 -> 2 [S: FINAL_STATUS; R23].
5. In one sentence for an examiner: "A scope-explicit fault-cost checker and a data set showing that redundancy verdicts are properties of (netlist, consumer, counting rule), not of the Verilog." That is a methods/measurement project, honest and defensible, but currently small.

Honest gap between where we are and "wow": the data are about toy designs we wrote; the tool answers a question nobody has yet asked it on a design someone else cares about.

## 2. Three strategic options

### Option A: "Redundancy linter": which of my redundancy copies are merged, share a decision net, and what does each consumer's fault cost
**Bigger claim (scoped, no novelty claim):** Given a netlist (or RTL synthesised through a named flow) and a declared consumer (which bits/nets downstream logic trusts), the tool prints (i) which declared redundant copies share nets after synthesis (structural, no solver), (ii) the minimum flips per consumer, exact within the bound, (iii) the cheapest witness, in plain words, and (iv) the netlist hash and counting rule so the number cannot be quoted out of context. It is M1 generalised from "mask of the result word" to "any consumer".
**Evidence already in hand:**
- Per-consumer cost is real and large: V1 = 1 flip for every non-colliding mask; V2 spike 1 to 3; full-word 3 (spike) / 2 (apt-0.33) [S: M1 sec 2]. 1785 cells, 0 unknowns, brute-force check agrees on 255/255 masks at T=4, k<=2 [S: M1 sec 1].
- Closed-form rule for the spike V2 (255/255) [S: M1 sec 3]; negative result: the rule does not transfer to re-synthesised netlists (68 mismatches) [S: M1 sec 3]. This is exactly why a structural sharing detector is needed [I].
- Synthesis sensitivity data: 864 configs, 2592 runs, deterministic; min 3 appears only with plain flow, abc_then_flatten, no opt_merge (+ conda 0.38 default/no-MUX/AND,OR, or no ABC) [S: E2 sec 1, 3]. Which stems are shared is visible: the 2-flip attacks use shared stems of fanout 2-3 [S: E2 sec 4; FINAL_STATUS].
- Product CLI with SARIF/JUnit/exit codes, Dockerfile and GH Action already drafted but never run [S: R24 sec 0, 4; product/ listing].
**Build in 6 h (tonight, feasible before 04:30 UTC):** a `structure` pass over the Yosys JSON: for each declared copy group, compute the fan-in cone of each result bit; list shared gates, their fanout, and the single-flip "dominator" nets (nets whose flip alone can change all consumer bits). Output a table and a plain-English sentence. Wire the M1 mask machinery into the product CLI as `--consumer-bits 0x0F` (the per-mask solve is a few seconds; 255 masks x 6 designs ran) and emit the "cost vs consumer width" curve. Add the figure `m1_flips_vs_mask.png` to the front page. [I: effort estimate]
**Build in 24 h:** consumer given as a small expression over nets (not just a mask: "ok = AND of bits 0..3", "alarm silent"); declared-copy-group annotation (comments or TOML); validation harness: structural prediction vs solver on all 864 E2 netlists x selected masks, reported as agreement rate; run on 3 third-party designs already fetched by R20 (CRC compare, sbox DMR, UART) so the demo is not only our toys [S: R20 designs exist].
**Build in 72 h:** scale work (cone-of-influence slicing and flop-only unrolling so 200-2000 gate designs run k=3), a CI badge showing the scoped verdict (never "secure"), a worked tutorial on one real Tiny Tapeout or OpenTitan-lite block (needs sv2v path; R20 flags this as a blocker), PyPI/Docker publication if the owner decides on name/licence.
**Risk:** (1) structural prediction may not match the solver (logic masking, reconvergent paths): then the linter degrades to "solver wrapper with a nice report" [I]; (2) overlap with SYNFI/k-FRP "per-output fault effect" reporting is probably larger than we know, as k-FRP full text is still unread [S: R23]; (3) 72 h scale target may fail (k=3 on 2040 gates did not finish) [S: R20].
**Why it impresses:**
- (a) ECE evaluator: a sharp, testable statement with a validation table (prediction vs exact solve), a negative result stated up front, and a clean separation of model from measurement. It is checkable in one afternoon.
- (b) Hardware engineer: it answers a question they actually have after synthesis ("did my TMR survive ABC, and does my downstream consumer trust one net?"), as a lint rule with a witness, in CI, exit code, SARIF [S: R24 positioning]. Credibility depends on a non-toy example.
- (c) Layperson: "It tells you whether your three locks are really three locks, and shows the one wire they secretly share." Good one-sentence story; the width-of-check curve is a simple graphic ("a guard who only looks at the front of the badge").

### Option B: Benchmark + leaderboard of countermeasure patterns across synthesis flows
**Bigger claim:** a public, versioned corpus (pattern x flow x fault-site rule x counting rule x consumer) with solver-checked minimum-flip numbers, where "pattern" = V0/V1/V2/V2m/V2s/V3, DMR S-box, CRC with TMR flag, sparse-FSM, redundant counter. A leaderboard ranks patterns by the worst case over flows, not by the best case.
**Evidence in hand:** E2's 864 x 3 repeated syntheses with SHA-256 per netlist, 0 failures, deterministic [S: E2 sec 1]; B2's 360-cell scope matrix [S: B2]; M1's 1785-cell consumer matrix [S: M1]; R20 designs with measured scaling (sbox_dmr k=2 minimum 2 in 46 s) [S: R20 sec 3, 5]; R21 lists real countermeasure patterns to encode (sparse FSM, redundant counters, mubi, anchor buffers) but all RTL is untested [S: R21 sec 1].
**6 h:** unify the three matrices into one CSV schema + one static HTML leaderboard (sortable, worst-case column first). **24 h:** add 4 to 6 new patterns (sparse FSM with Hamming distance d, double/cross counter, DMR with one comparator vs per-bit), run through 4 flows. **72 h:** a `make bench` target, contribution template (one Verilog file + a goal file), and a result-schema/versioning doc, with Docker-pinned Yosys because 0.33 vs 0.38 give different verdicts [S: R24 #3].
**Risk:** a leaderboard needs participants and is only as meaningful as its patterns; our patterns are self-authored toys, so it can read as "we ranked our own homework" [I]. Flow variance (E2) is mostly about ABC details with the cause not located [S: E2 sec 4]; the leaderboard would rank noise unless the cause is understood. Highest cost in compute and curation, lowest cost in new theory.
**Why it impresses:** (a) ECE: breadth, a dataset, a reproducibility discipline (hashes, determinism); but "what does it show?" must be answerable. (b) Hardware engineer: useful as a pattern zoo and for choosing flow flags; weak unless the patterns resemble real ones. (c) Layperson: a scoreboard is instantly readable; the surprise "same design, three scores" is the story; moderate.

### Option C: Fix suggester: find the weak spot, change the RTL, re-verify
**Bigger claim:** the tool does not just say "fails"; it proposes a change from a small, verified menu (separate decision nets per consumer bit, raise the idle-to-accept Hamming distance on the bits the consumer checks, keep/anchor attributes so copies survive synthesis, move the decision into hardened voter) and re-runs the solver to report the new minimum with before/after hashes.
**Evidence in hand:** M1 derived a floor: min flips <= Hamming distance from idle states to accept word on the checked bits, so "make that distance large" is a concrete, testable lever [S: M1 sec 5(c)]. NEGATIVE evidence that matters: naive majority voting (V2m) lowered the full-word figure from 3 to 2 and a shared voter (V2s) is 1 for every mask; "voting did NOT raise the best case" [S: M1 sec 5]. V3 reached 3 in all finished cells but cells are unfinished, search capped at k<=3, not shown secure [S: B2; DS F7]. OpenTitan already recommends sparse encodings and anchors [S: R21 sec 1], so the lever is known, only its automatic verified application is ours.
**6 h:** hand-apply the Hamming-distance lever to V2 (change accept code so idle-to-accept distance on 8 bits is >=4) and measure with the existing M1 mask harness; this single experiment shows whether the lever works at all. **24 h:** a rewriter limited to ONE template (parametrised accept-code and per-bit decision gating) on the 8-bit family, with a verification loop and a before/after report. **72 h:** generalise to FSMs with declared state registers (sparse-recode state, check distance), still bounded; a "fix did not help" outcome is reported as a first-class result.
**Risk:** highest. RTL rewriting for arbitrary designs is a research project of its own; M1 already shows the obvious fix is wrong; a fixer that sometimes lowers security is worse than none; output "bounded k<=3" invites over-trust. Likely stays a template on toy designs [I].
**Why it impresses:** (a) ECE: a closed loop (detect, fix, re-verify) is the shape of a real VLSI deliverable and a before/after number is clean; the negative result (voting hurts) shows rigor. (b) Hardware engineer: only if the fix is non-obvious and correct; a Hamming-distance suggestion they already know will read as trivial. (c) Layperson: highest wow ("it found the weak spot and mended it"), the most demonstrable.
